Fly pursuit

A male fruit fly chasing a female keeps her in front of him with a small set of neurons that detect small objects and steer. Here a circuit of 265 neurons taken from the fly connectome turns a simulated robot dog toward a target, or the real robot dog's head toward a face its camera sees.

Simulator, and the robot's head. In the simulator the dog, its camera and the target are simulated. On the robot only the head turns, toward a face seen by the camera in its mouth; the legs do not walk. With our firmware module the robot can run the circuit on its own board. On the robot.

Eye to turn

How it works

A camera stands in for the fly's eye. The circuit runs on the laptop; the simulated dog is the body.

Camera

Reports where the target is, as an angle left or right of the nose. 15 frames a second, 100 ms late, a little noise, and only inside its field of view. All assumed.
→bearing

Pursuit circuit

LC10a detectors in each eye feed AOTU019 and AOTU025, which act on the two DNa02 steering neurons. Left minus right DNa02, times one fitted gain, is the turn command.
→turn

Dog

Turns in place, at most 90 degrees a second, with a short lag. Its new heading changes what the camera sees next.
NeuronsWhat they do
LC10aSmall-object detectors, about 115 per eye. Each one watches a small patch of the visual field.
AOTU019One per side. Inhibits the opposite side's steering neuron. Driven by targets near the front.
AOTU025One per side. Excites the same side's steering neuron. Driven by targets to the side.
DNa02Steering neurons, one per side. The difference between them sets the turn.

A target on the left raises the left DNa02 (through AOTU025) and lowers the right one (through AOTU019), so the dog turns left. The circuit also holds the other cell types between LC10a and DNa02 (AOTU015a, CB3127, CB2070, LAL027) and two local inhibitory types (TuTuAa, AOTU041): 265 neurons in all.

Measured, assumed, fitted

from the connectome

Which neurons connect, how many synapses, and whether each connection excites or inhibits (from the predicted transmitter). FlyWire, FAFB v783.

assumed

Where in the visual field each LC10a looks (estimated from its inputs), its tuning width, the time constant, a resting drive for DNa02, how arousal acts, and every camera and robot number.

fitted

One number: the output gain, turn per unit of DNa02 difference. Each simple baseline also has one fitted number.

This is one fly's brain, and its two halves are not equal. Run as it is, the circuit keeps the target about 17 degrees to the right. The default controller runs it twice, once with the target's bearing and once with its mirror image, and averages the two. Nothing is fitted for this.

Rewired is the mean of 5 networks and random of 3. Every circuit variant uses the connectome's fitted gain. Numbers come from packages/flybrain-models/src/flybrain_models/pursuit_benchmark.json.

What this shows. With one fitted number, the fly's wiring steers toward the target and settles after almost every jump. Shuffle the wiring or randomise it and it no longer pursues. Without AOTU019 it turns away from targets near the front; without AOTU025 it turns more weakly toward targets at the side, but still faster than the robot can turn, so little is lost.

What it does not show. It is not better than a simple rule. A proportional controller with one tuned number tracks slightly better (13.8 against 14.3 degrees). The circuit is worth showing for what it reveals about the fly, not as a better controller.

Try it

One command, no robot needed.

uv sync
uv run fly-pursuit --mock --open

The browser opens on the arena with the target 20 degrees to the left. Drag it around the ring, or click the arena and use the arrow keys. Then try these:

  1. Switch off AOTU019

    Pick Without AOTU019, field of view ±30°, target Jumps. Targets near the front are now pushed away: the dog turns until the target leaves its view. The steering curve points the wrong way near 0 degrees.

  2. One fly's brain, as it is

    Pick Connectome, single brain with the target still in front. The dog settles with the target about 17 degrees to its right. Switch back to the mirror-averaged connectome and it centres the target.

  3. Arousal 0

    With the connectome, drag the arousal slider to 0. That silences AOTU019, with the same result as removing it. Raise it slowly and watch the AOTU019 neurons and the middle of the steering curve come back.

  4. Shuffle the wiring

    Rewired keeps the neurons and the number of synapses between cell types; Random keeps only the size. Neither pursues.

  5. A simple rule

    Proportional turns in proportion to the bearing; Bang-bang turns at full rate outside a dead zone. Press Reset numbers and compare their running numbers with the connectome's on the same target motion.

On the robot

The camera sits in the dog's mouth and turns with the head, so the head is the body the circuit turns. The legs do not walk. Four ways to run it, in the order to try them.

The default, --camera auto, asks the robot which camera module it has and uses ours if it is there, the stock one otherwise, and says which. --onboard without our module stops with a message naming firmware/bittle/fly_pursuit.

Measured on the robot with the module's own commands (its README, 2026-11-18): over USB, 15.4 results a second, none skipped; on-board with a face about 50 cm away moving side to side, a face in 385 of 481 results (80 %), the head between -36 and +41 degrees for a face between -42 and +43 degrees, the face a median 3.0 degrees from the image centre (90th percentile 8.0). Over Bluetooth, 463 results in 30 s (15.4 a second), none missing, a face in 378 (82 %), median 1.2 and 90th percentile 9.8 degrees from the centre. This program's on-board and module modes have so far been tested only with a fake robot, and no session has been longer than a minute. The line format is in the module's README, "Host lines".

Before you start

  • The robot on the floor, away from edges, battery on. It sits when the demo starts.
  • The camera in the Grove socket labelled SCL SDA +5V GND, not the one by TX2/RX2 (except on its own cable).
  • Stock module over Bluetooth: switch the robot off and on first if its camera was used since. It can start the camera only once per power-on; a second start reports only the last old position, which looks like a still face. Over USB the program restarts the board itself. Our module needs none of this.
  • Camera on its USB cable with the robot on USB too: give --port or --camera-port, since both ports look the same to the computer.

Run it

uv run fly-pursuit --ble --onboard --open
uv run fly-pursuit --port /dev/cu.usbserial-XXXX --onboard --open
uv run fly-pursuit --ble --camera module --open
uv run fly-pursuit --ble --camera robot --open
uv run fly-pursuit --ble --camera usb --open

On macOS, Bluetooth from a terminal goes through uv run flybrain-bittle ble-run -- uv run fly-pursuit --ble --onboard --open. In Flydeck, pick the robot instead of the simulator, then tick "the robot computes" or choose the camera connection. Stop with Ctrl-C or Stop.

  1. Start the robot sits

    Voice module off (sounds could make the robot move). On-board: our module on, then its on-board control on; the firmware makes the robot sit. Our module with the laptop computing: the module on (it does not move the robot), then the robot is told to sit. Stock module: camera on with its own head reaction off, then asked to report the face whenever it moves; a missing reply is not taken as a failure, because over Bluetooth replies go missing while that camera runs. Own cable: the robot is told to sit and the camera is asked for a result every frame.

  2. Follow the head turns

    Each camera result gives the face's bearing, from its place in the 240-pixel image and a field of view of 60 degrees across (not properly measured; one test gave about 57). The camera turns with the head, so the bearing is corrected for how far the head has turned since. The circuit turns the bearing into a turn command; the head turns by it, at most 60 degrees a second and 50 degrees each way. On-board the robot does all of this once per camera result and the laptop sends the head no command; otherwise the laptop does it about 15 times a second, times a gain (default 1), with at most 8 head commands a second.

  3. No face the head holds

    With our module and on its own cable "no face" is reported every frame and counts as lost at once. Through the stock module a still face sends nothing: a face within 8 degrees of centre stays "seen", one further out counts as lost after 1 second. After 3 seconds lost the head holds where it is; it does not search.

  4. Stop the robot rests

    Always, on Stop, Ctrl-C, an error or a lost link, sending every step even if a reply is missing: on-board control off (on-board), head back to centre, camera off (our module, or the stock one, which the robot then saves) or the camera's results stopped (own cable), voice back on, IMU updates back on (stock module), rest. After a lost link the program reconnects once to do this.

On-board: what the circuit panel shows

The robot does not send its neurons' activity. The panel runs the same circuit on the laptop with the bearing the robot reports, for display, and says so. The robot's own turn command is the one its firmware prints; the dashboard shows it next to the recomputed one and their difference. The controller, arousal and gain cannot be changed: it is the connectome circuit at arousal 1.

Warnings

With our module a line on the dashboard shows whether the camera runs, how many results came, how many are missing between sequence numbers, and every error count. If the module reports that the camera's AI side is not answering or the bus is held, it says: power-cycle the camera, switch the robot off and on. If nothing answers on the socket, it says the camera is not plugged in there. No result at all in 20 seconds also raises a warning.

If the stock camera module could not be switched off, or the head moves by itself after a run: the robot saves which modules are on and restarts them at power-on, that camera with its own head reaction. Switch the robot off, unplug the camera, switch it on, then run uv run fly-pursuit --camera-off --port /dev/cu.usbserial-XXXX (or --ble). Our module needs no recovery: started at power-on it only reports and does not move the robot, unless you switched on "follow a face after power-on" (below). --camera-off switches that off too.

Robot options: --onboard, --camera auto, module, robot or usb, --camera-port, --gain 1 (0.1 to 2), --camera-fov 60, --head-rate 60 deg/s, --pan-limit 50 deg (at most 120, the firmware's limit), --command-rate 8 per second, --pan-sign 1, --no-usb-restart, --leave-voice-off, --camera-off, --autostart on or off. On-board the laptop's controller settings are ignored. Limits: short runs only so far; the stock camera module's measured problems (once per power-on, no "no face", the IMU stopped, lost replies over Bluetooth, once a stuck I2C bus) are in packages/flybrain-edge/NOTES.md.

The robot by itself

The newer build of our firmware module (firmware/bittle/fly_pursuit) can save a "follow a face after power-on" setting. With it on, a few seconds after every power-on and with no computer connected, the robot beeps three times, sits and turns its head toward a face by itself, with the circuit on its own board.

Safety. Once this is on, the robot moves every time it is switched on. Switch it on only on the floor, away from edges, and expect the three beeps, then the sit.

Switch it on or off

uv run fly-pursuit --ble --autostart on
uv run fly-pursuit --ble --autostart off

Or in Flydeck: pick the robot, set "Robot: follow a face after power-on" to on or off, Start. "leave as it is" is a normal run. The program connects, saves the setting, rests the robot and exits, with one line saying what is saved and what the robot will do at the next power-on. --camera-off also switches it off.

What it needs

The newer build of the module. An older build does not know this setting; the program then says the robot's module is older and has no autostart, and that the newer build must be flashed. A normal run of the demo does not change the saved setting: if the robot is already following a face by itself, the demo takes over and leaves the setting as it was. Run on a Bittle X on 2026-11-18: both directions of the setting over Bluetooth, the robot's own start after a restart over USB (not after the power switch), and the demo taking over a robot that was following by itself.

Not yet

What is missing, in the simulator and on the robot.

Not built

  • On the robot only the head turns; a lost face is not searched for. This program has run on the robot with the stock camera module; its on-board and our-module modes, and the camera on its own cable, have only been tested with fakes. No session longer than a minute.
  • No forward walking: the dog only turns in place.
  • The ±100° setting stands for a camera on a panning head; the head is not simulated.

Not in the model

  • Simulator camera numbers are assumed: frame rate, delay, noise, dropped frames, field of view. The turn limit and lag too. On the robot the field of view is still assumed.
  • No direction selectivity: real LC10a respond to small moving objects; here any visible target drives them.
  • No elevation, object size or contrast; arousal is a fixed level, not a state that builds up.
