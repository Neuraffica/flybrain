Fly pursuit

A male fruit fly chasing a female keeps her in front of him with a small set of neurons that detect small objects and steer. Here a circuit of 265 neurons taken from the fly connectome turns a simulated robot dog toward a target.

Simulator only, for now. The dog, its camera and the target are simulated. The robot version needs the camera's frame rate, delay and field of view measured first.

What you will see

One dashboard, in the browser or in Flydeck's window.

The chase

The dog sits in the middle of a ring, seen from above. Drag the target around the ring and the dog turns to face it. The shaded wedge is what its camera can see; outside it the dog is blind.

02 · the circuit

Eye to turn

The neurons light up as the signal passes through: small-object detectors in each eye, two neurons per side that push or hold back steering, and the two steering neurons whose difference sets the turn.

Change the wiring

Switch off one cell type, flip its sign, shuffle the connections, or replace the whole circuit with a simple rule. Each change shows in the steering curve and in the running numbers.

How it works

A camera stands in for the fly's eye. The circuit runs on the laptop; the simulated dog is the body.

Camera

Reports where the target is, as an angle left or right of the nose. 15 frames a second, 100 ms late, a little noise, and only inside its field of view. All assumed.
→bearing

Pursuit circuit

LC10a detectors in each eye feed AOTU019 and AOTU025, which act on the two DNa02 steering neurons. Left minus right DNa02, times one fitted gain, is the turn command.
→turn

Dog

Turns in place, at most 90 degrees a second, with a short lag. Its new heading changes what the camera sees next.
NeuronsWhat they do
LC10aSmall-object detectors, about 115 per eye. Each one watches a small patch of the visual field.
AOTU019One per side. Inhibits the opposite side's steering neuron. Driven by targets near the front.
AOTU025One per side. Excites the same side's steering neuron. Driven by targets to the side.
DNa02Steering neurons, one per side. The difference between them sets the turn.

A target on the left raises the left DNa02 (through AOTU025) and lowers the right one (through AOTU019), so the dog turns left. The circuit also holds the other cell types between LC10a and DNa02 (AOTU015a, CB3127, CB2070, LAL027) and two local inhibitory types (TuTuAa, AOTU041): 265 neurons in all.

Measured, assumed, fitted

from the connectome

Which neurons connect, how many synapses, and whether each connection excites or inhibits (from the predicted transmitter). FlyWire, FAFB v783.

assumed

Where in the visual field each LC10a looks (estimated from its inputs), its tuning width, the time constant, a resting drive for DNa02, how arousal acts, and every camera and robot number.

fitted

One number: the output gain, turn per unit of DNa02 difference. Each simple baseline also has one fitted number.

This is one fly's brain, and its two halves are not equal. Run as it is, the circuit keeps the target about 17 degrees to the right. The default controller runs it twice, once with the target's bearing and once with its mirror image, and averages the two. Nothing is fitted for this.

Rewired is the mean of 5 networks and random of 3. Every circuit variant uses the connectome's fitted gain. Numbers come from packages/flybrain-models/src/flybrain_models/pursuit_benchmark.json.

What this shows. With one fitted number, the fly's wiring steers toward the target and settles after almost every jump. Shuffle the wiring or randomise it and it no longer pursues. Without AOTU019 it turns away from targets near the front; without AOTU025 it turns more weakly toward targets at the side, but still faster than the robot can turn, so little is lost.

What it does not show. It is not better than a simple rule. A proportional controller with one tuned number tracks slightly better (13.8 against 14.3 degrees). The circuit is worth showing for what it reveals about the fly, not as a better controller.

Try it

One command, no robot needed.

uv sync
uv run fly-pursuit --mock --open

The browser opens on the arena with the target 20 degrees to the left. Drag it around the ring, or click the arena and use the arrow keys. Then try these:

  1. Switch off AOTU019

    Pick Without AOTU019, field of view ±30°, target Jumps. Targets near the front are now pushed away: the dog turns until the target leaves its view. The steering curve points the wrong way near 0 degrees.

  2. One fly's brain, as it is

    Pick Connectome, single brain with the target still in front. The dog settles with the target about 17 degrees to its right. Switch back to the mirror-averaged connectome and it centres the target.

  3. Arousal 0

    With the connectome, drag the arousal slider to 0. That silences AOTU019, with the same result as removing it. Raise it slowly and watch the AOTU019 neurons and the middle of the steering curve come back.

  4. Shuffle the wiring

    Rewired keeps the neurons and the number of synapses between cell types; Random keeps only the size. Neither pursues.

  5. A simple rule

    Proportional turns in proportion to the bearing; Bang-bang turns at full rate outside a dead zone. Press Reset numbers and compare their running numbers with the connectome's on the same target motion.

Not yet

This first version only simulates.

Not built

  • No robot link. --port and --ble are refused.
  • No forward walking: the dog only turns in place.
  • The ±100° setting stands for a camera on a panning head; the head is not simulated.

Not in the model

  • Camera numbers are assumed until measured: frame rate, delay, noise, dropped frames, field of view. The turn limit and lag too.
  • No direction selectivity: real LC10a respond to small moving objects; here any visible target drives them.
  • No elevation, object size or contrast; arousal is a fixed level, not a state that builds up.
