Fly compass

151 neurons from a real fruit fly's brain, wired exactly as they are in the fly, remember which way a robot dog is facing. The dog's arm points there like a compass needle.

Partly checked on a real Bittle X+Arm. Needle and sweep modes have run on it: it stands, turns its head and arm, and steps left and right by itself, with the arm seen holding its direction while the body turns. Hold mode also works, stopping about 12° from the target. It also runs over Bluetooth with no cable, through the Mac helper described below.

What you will see

Three things happen at once: on the table, and on a live dashboard on the laptop.

01 · on the table

The needle

Turn the dog by hand. Its head and arm swing the other way, so the arm keeps pointing at the direction it had when you started, up to 80° either side.

02 · on the screen

The bump

A ring of 47 neurons, one dot each. A patch of them is active. As the dog turns, the patch travels around the ring. That patch is the fly's sense of direction.

03 · the experiment

The blindfold

Press B. The model stops receiving the heading. The real wiring keeps a bump alive; the same neurons with scrambled wiring lose theirs within seconds.

How it works

The model runs on the laptop. The dog is the body: it reports its orientation and moves its head.

Bittle X

Its motion sensor reports which way it faces, about 18 times a second over USB, or over Bluetooth.
→heading

Fly circuit on the laptop

151 neurons and 2,665 connections of the head-direction circuit, taken from the FlyWire map of a fly brain. The heading is shown to the ring as a landmark; the ring's own wiring holds it.
→angle

Head and arm

The head joint turns against the body so the arm stays on target.

Two other models get exactly the same input and are scored beside it: a scrambled control (same neurons, same number of connections each, wiring shuffled) and a standard filter (the usual engineering answer: add up the turn rate, correct toward the measured heading).

What to expect, in numbers

Measured in simulation before the demo was built. Lower error is better.

ModelRemembers heading for 60 s in the darkStill has a bump after 60 sError following the cueError with turn rate only
Fly wiring58%100%14°85°
Scrambled wiring5%27%10°86°
Random wiring0%0%10°81°
No wiring at all0%0%6°82°
Standard filter100%n/a0°4°

"Remembers" means the bump is within 30° of the last landmark, over 24 starting headings. Scrambled is the mean of 5 shuffles, random of 3. A model that never moves scores 82° in the last column.

What this shows. The memory comes from the fly's wiring. Take the wiring away, shuffle it, or randomise it, and the heading is gone within about 5 seconds.

What it does not show. This is not a better compass. The memory is coarse: the bump settles into a few preferred positions, so the average error is about 43°. The circuit cannot follow a turn from turn rate alone, and the standard filter is more accurate on every measure. On the dashboard, expect the fly-wiring needle to sit 10 to 25° off while you turn the dog.

Try it without the robot

Everything runs against a simulated dog. Do this first; it takes a minute.

uv sync
uv run fly-compass --mock

Open http://127.0.0.1:8765. Drag the dial or press ← → to turn the simulated dog. Press B for the blindfold.

Scripted: memory

Turns 90°, blindfolds, holds still for 20 s, then gives the cue back.

uv run fly-compass --mock --mock-script memory

In the test run the fly wiring kept a strong bump (confidence 0.93) while the scrambled control's faded (0.22). The fly bump slid about 70° from the target: this heading is one of the 42% it does not hold.

Scripted: dark turn

Blindfolds, then turns 90° in the dark.

uv run fly-compass --mock --mock-script dark-turn

Both ring models lose track. The standard filter follows the turn. This is the case the fly circuit fails.

With the real dog

Go in this order. Each step only moves on to more motion once the one before looks right.

Before you start

  • Bittle X with the arm, battery charged and switched on. USB alone does not power the servos.
  • A USB-C data cable to this Mac. Bluetooth also works, but start with USB.
  • A flat table, with nothing within reach of the arm as it swings 80° to each side.
  • Firmware built as "Bittle X+Arm". The app warns at start if it sees something else.

  1. Probe no movement

    Asks the dog for its motion-sensor reading and prints the raw reply. Nothing moves.

    uv run flybrain-bittle probe

    You want the verdict line to say OK. If it does not, copy the output: it shows exactly what your firmware sent.

  2. Check the turn direction no movement

    uv run flybrain-bittle probe --rotate-test

    Turn the dog to its left by hand when asked. The app already allows for this firmware counting clockwise as positive (measured on a real dog). If the heading on the dashboard still goes the wrong way, add --yaw-sign 1 to every command below.

  3. Observe stands, then still

    uv run fly-compass --mode observe

    The dog stands up. For the first 5 seconds do not touch it: the app measures how fast the motion sensor drifts and subtracts that. Then open the dashboard and turn the dog by hand: the white pointer and the bump should follow.

  4. Needle head and arm move

    uv run fly-compass

    Turn the dog slowly. The arm should keep pointing where it pointed at the start. If it turns the wrong way, stop and add --pan-sign -1. Press Re-zero on the dashboard to pick a new target.

  5. Blindfold head and arm move

    Hold the dog still and press B. Watch the two rings: the fly wiring should keep its bump, the scrambled one should fade. Then turn the dog while blindfolded: the ring models lose the heading, and the arm goes with them.

  6. Hold heading, optional the dog walks

    uv run fly-compass --mode hold --i-have-space

    The dog steps in place to turn itself back when it is more than 20° off. Put it on the floor for this one.

  7. Sweep, optional the dog walks

    uv run fly-compass --mode sweep --i-have-space

    Nobody touches the dog. It steps in place to the left until it faces 35° left of where it started, stands for 3 seconds, steps round to 35° right, and keeps going. The arm should keep pointing at the start direction while the body swings under it. Put it on the floor with about a metre clear around it. Turning is slow on carpet, a few degrees a second, so one swing can take half a minute. If a turn takes too long the dog gives up and turns back; after three of those it stops walking and stays in needle mode.

To stop: press Ctrl-C in the terminal. The dog lies down and its servos switch off. If the cable comes out mid-run nothing can be sent, and the dog stays in its last pose until you switch it off.

Over Bluetooth

uv run flybrain-bittle ble-run -- uv run fly-compass --ble

On a Mac, start Bluetooth commands through this small helper. Started straight from Terminal, macOS stops them the moment they reach for Bluetooth, with no message. The first time, macOS asks whether "Flybrain BLE" may use Bluetooth: allow it. Output still shows in the terminal and Ctrl-C still lays the dog down. The firmware also makes the dog lie down whenever a Bluetooth connection opens; the app stands it up again.

Replay a session

uv run fly-compass --replay runs/<stamp>

Every run is logged under runs/. Replay feeds it back through the models with no robot attached.

What has not been checked on a real dog

The link was written from the firmware's source code and has since been run on one real dog. These are the things still to confirm.

Likely to need a flag

  • Whether 80° of swing is clear of the arm's cables. Sweep at 25° used about 45°.
  • Other firmware versions: turn directions were confirmed on B10_250330 only.

Unknown until tried

  • How smooth the head looks at 15 commands a second.
  • Long sessions: the board's heading counter grows without limit. The app copes past 10,000°, but nobody has run it for a whole day.
  • Why the dog turns right much more slowly than left. It made one hold turn hit its 6-second limit.
  • Sweep on a hard floor, and at angles above 25°. On carpet it ran two cycles with no stalls.
  • Sweep over Bluetooth after the stop fix. Once, over Bluetooth, the "stand still" command was lost and the dog kept turning through the pause. The app now waits for the dog to confirm each command, and if the dog keeps turning after a stop it sends the stop again, then lays the dog down.

The Petoi camera is not used. The firmware can report where a detected object is and how big it looks, but not pictures. Using that position as a real visual landmark for the ring is the natural next step.
